What this is

This is Rung 4 of a ladder of tests toward computing by geometry (small networks that read by settling into stored patterns, with no trained model) instead of by large models. Rung 2 showed a settling cell can improve itself with no model. Rung 4 asks what happens when nodes share what they learned: does a shape that one node earned help another, and can the receiver stay safe while it takes in shapes from outside?

The rule B follows is "trust the key, check the shape". A valid signature from a trusted key only gets a share in the door. B still has to see it win on B's own exam before it changes anything. What the other rungs found, held and failed, is on the ladder page.

How it works

Step by step

  1. Two nodes. Both start from Rung 1's cell. A runs Rung 2's loop for 600 cycles on its half of the MNIST training digits; B runs it for only 60 on the other half.
  2. A shares. A's shape as exact bytes, A's Ed25519 signature over those bytes, A's public key. The key is seeded, so every signature re-derives byte for byte.
  3. B checks before reading. The key must be on B's roster and the signature must verify over the raw bytes before anything is parsed. A changed byte, a cut-short file, a signature from another key or over other bytes: all stop here.
  4. B parses. The bytes must build a cell in the exact whole-number range. Signed junk stops here.
  5. B tests. A fresh exam from B's own data: the shared shape must get at least two more right than it newly gets wrong. A validly signed bad shape stops here.
  6. Judge once. B alone and B after were frozen by hash; then all 10,000 test digits were read once.

Blueprint

The order things happened in, as the git history shows it

    Adoption

    For a business

    Sites, branches or devices can share what each one learned without a central model or a vendor in the middle. Each site keeps the final say, tested on its own data.

    For engineering

    Verify before parse on raw bytes, then a local acceptance test, then adopt. Every refusal carries its reason; every signature re-derives from a seed; the whole trade re-runs in a browser.

    For risk and procurement

    A stolen key alone cannot push a worse shape into a node: any shape still has to win the node's own exam. It could push in one that wins, so keys still need guarding. Here A and B run in one process and nothing is sent anywhere else; a real deployment needs its own network design. The page loads only its own files.

    A pilot, step by step

    1. Pick two sites that do the same task, with a right answer you can check later.
    2. Write down the bars before anything is built, and seal them.
    3. Give each site a key (generated on the site and kept secret: the keys on this page are derived from public seeds only so that anyone can re-run the record), and put both keys on each site's roster.
    4. Let each site improve on its own past cases, then trade shapes, each checking the other's on its own data.
    5. Freeze, then judge once on held-out cases from the receiving site. The fee is due only if it beat your current way.
    6. Then run it beside your current process, deciding nothing, before it is trusted with anything.

    What it runs on: Node.js 20 or a current browser. No GPU, no cloud service, no model download.

    White paper: sharing learned shapes without trusting them

    The question. Federated systems share learned parameters and usually trust the sender. Rung 4 asks whether small settling cells can share what they learn in a way where the receiver gains from honest shares and loses nothing to forged or bad ones.

    The method. Two nodes on disjoint halves of MNIST's training digits, both starting from Rung 1's cell, both improving with Rung 2's loop for very different budgets. One signed exchange, five forgeries and one honest-but-bad share, all sealed in advance. The exchange and B's shapes were frozen by hash; then B was judged once on the 10,000 test digits.

      The limits, plainly:

      • Two nodes and one exchange. A mesh of many nodes under loss is Rung 6.
      • The test digits were read before by Rung 3, for a different cell and task; nothing in this repository had read them.
      • B's final shape is A's shape; the gain is the value of A's longer run, delivered safely. B does not merge shapes.
      • Not a defence against a stolen key's genuine-looking data (Rung 5), and not on a chip (Rung 7).

      Questions buyers ask

      What if a key is stolen?The thief can sign, but a signed shape still has to win the receiving node's own exam before anything changes. The honest-but-bad share here was validly signed and was refused. Defending against a stolen key's genuine-looking inputs is Rung 5.
      Does sharing move our data?No. Only the shape moves: a short text of patterns. Each site's own data never leaves it; the exam a site uses to judge a share is its own.
      How would we test it on our own sites?Seal the bars, run the loop at each site on its own data, share signed shapes, freeze, and judge once on held-out data. The fee is due only if it beat your current way.
      Our inputs are personal data. What about UK GDPR?Data stays where it is; only patterns are shared between your own sites. Your own data protection officer still signs off the use; no legal sign-off is claimed here.
